# Existentialism and Phenomenology: The Study of Being and Experience

## I. Phenomenology: The Return to the Things Themselves

Phenomenology is the study of structures of consciousness as experienced from the first-person point of view.

### 1. Edmund Husserl and Epoché
Husserl proposed the **phenomenological reduction** or *epoché*—the act of suspending judgment about the existence of the external world to focus purely on the way things appear to consciousness.

### 2. Martin Heidegger: Dasein and Being-in-the-World
Heidegger shifted phenomenology toward ontology (the study of being). He replaced the "detached subject" with **Dasein** ("being-there").
- **Thrownness (Geworfenheit):** We find ourselves already in a world with existing meanings and structures.
- **Authenticity:** The courage to face one's own mortality (*Being-towards-death*) and take ownership of one's existence.

## II. Existentialism: Existence Precedes Essence

Existentialism posits that humans are not born with a predefined nature or purpose; rather, we define ourselves through our choices.

### 1. Jean-Paul Sartre: Radical Freedom
Sartre argued that for humans, **existence precedes essence**. Because there is no divine designer, we are "condemned to be free."
- **Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi):** The act of lying to oneself to avoid the anxiety of absolute freedom by pretending one is defined by a role or destiny.

### 2. Søren Kierkegaard: The Leap of Faith
The father of existentialism, Kierkegaard focused on the subjective experience of anxiety and the "absurd." He argued that the most profound truths are found not through reason, but through a passionate, subjective commitment—the "leap of faith."
