# Analytical Philosophy and Language: The Linguistic Turn

## I. The Quest for Logical Precision

Analytical philosophy emerged in the early 20th century with the goal of clarifying concepts through the rigorous analysis of language.

### 1. Gottlob Frege and the Sense/Reference Distinction
Frege distinguished between:
- **Sinn (Sense):** The mode of presentation (e.g., "The Morning Star").
- **Bedeutung (Reference):** The actual object in the world (e.g., Venus).
This solved the problem of identity, explaining why "A = B" can be informative even if A and B refer to the same thing.

### 2. Bertrand Russell and the Theory of Descriptions
Russell argued that many philosophical problems are actually linguistic confusions. By analyzing "definite descriptions," he showed that statements about non-existent entities (e.g., "The present King of France") are logically false rather than meaningless.

## II. Ludwig Wittgenstein: Two Phases of Thought

Wittgenstein's influence is split into two distinct periods.

### 1. The Early Wittgenstein (Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus)
The "picture theory" of language. He argued that language maps to the world by mirroring the logical structure of facts. Anything that cannot be mapped—ethics, aesthetics, mysticism—is "unsayable."

### 2. The Later Wittgenstein (Philosophical Investigations)
He abandoned the picture theory for the concept of **Language Games**. Meaning is not a fixed map but is derived from **use** within a specific social context.
- **Family Resemblances:** Concepts are not defined by a single essential feature but by a network of overlapping similarities.

## III. Synthesis: Logic, Language, and Reality

The shift from formal logic in [[Logic-and-Epistemology]] to the fluid "language games" of the later Wittgenstein marks the evolution of how we perceive the relationship between the mind, the word, and the world.
