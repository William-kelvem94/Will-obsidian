# Logic and Epistemology: The Foundations of Knowledge and Reason

## I. Formal Logic: The Mechanics of Inference

Logic is the study of the forms of inference. A valid argument is one where the conclusion necessarily follows from the premises.

### 1. Deductive Reasoning
Deduction moves from the general to the specific. If the premises are true and the structure is valid, the conclusion *must* be true.
- **Modus Ponens:** If $P \rightarrow Q$ and $P$, then $Q$.
- **Modus Tollens:** If $P \rightarrow Q$ and $\neg Q$, then $\neg P$.

### 2. Inductive Reasoning
Induction moves from specific observations to general conclusions. Inductive arguments are probabilistic rather than certain.
- **Enumerative Induction:** Observing that all seen swans are white and concluding that "all swans are white."

## II. The Problem of Induction

David Hume famously argued that induction lacks a rational justification. We assume the future will resemble the past based on the fact that it has done so in the past—a circular argument. This "problem of induction" challenges the very foundation of empirical science.

## III. Epistemological Shifts: Popper and Kuhn

The 20th century saw a move away from simple inductivism toward more complex models of scientific growth.

### 1. Karl Popper: Falsificationism
Popper argued that science does not progress by proving theories true (verification), but by attempting to prove them false (**falsification**). A theory is only scientific if it is falsifiable. This avoids the trap of induction by focusing on the elimination of error.

### 2. Thomas Kuhn: Paradigm Shifts
Kuhn argued that science does not progress linearly. Instead, it operates within **paradigms** (sets of shared beliefs and methods). When anomalies accumulate that the current paradigm cannot explain, a **scientific revolution** occurs, leading to a paradigm shift.
